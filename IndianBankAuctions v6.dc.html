How a listing earns the word verified

Verified means somebody actually went there.

Every property on this portal passes through five hands before you see it: three of them are models, and two are human eyes that have to agree with each other.

01
Notice captured

The printed sale notice is scanned or taken from the publisher’s e-paper the morning it appears.

02
AI-read, human-corrected

The extraction model pulls lender, reserve, EMD, dates and schedule of property, scores its own confidence field by field, and a maker corrects anything it flagged.

03
Checked by a second person

A checker compares every field against the clipping, and publishes the photographs the bank released with it — no waiting on a field visit.

04
Assigned for a site visit

On approval the routing model assigns a field technician in that city by travel distance and how close the auction date is, with the address parsed from the notice.

05
Visited, then verified again

The technician uploads dated photographs, a walkthrough video and the GPS pin. AI screens them for blur, wrong façade and pin mismatch; the checker verifies what survives, and the listing is marked Verified.

The field engineer’s photographs, the walkthrough video and the GPS pin open with the ₹4,999 property report.

The visit costs us money to produce and it is the single thing that tells you whether the flat is occupied, whether the building is maintained and whether the address in the notice is even real. Free accounts see that a visit happened and when. ₹4,999 on that property shows you everything the engineer saw, plus the documents and the legal opinion.

Everything a first-time bidder has to know before the hammer.

How a bank comes to auction a house, what the SARFAESI Act lets it do and what it stops it from doing, which judgements protect you as the buyer, what you save against the open market, and the twelve things to check before you pay an EMD.

1 · Why a bank is selling somebody's house

A borrower mortgaged the property against a loan and then stopped paying. Once the account has been unpaid for 90 days the lender must classify it as a non-performing asset. It can then recover its money by selling the security — the property — under the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002, which everybody shortens to SARFAESI.

The bank is not a property developer and has no interest in holding real estate. It wants the outstanding dues, the interest and its costs — nothing more. That is the whole reason auction prices start below what the same building would fetch through a broker: the reserve price is set from a valuer's report against the debt, not from what the market's mood will bear.

You are not buying from the borrower. You are buying from a secured creditor exercising a statutory power of sale, and your title comes from a sale certificate issued by an authorised officer of the bank — a document that is registered and that flows from the statute rather than from the previous owner's willingness to sign.
